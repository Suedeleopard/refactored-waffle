# X Behaving Like Y

### A method for not sounding like everyone else in the room

*By Thad Cox*

---

## Before you start

This is short on purpose. You can read it in twenty minutes and use it the same afternoon.

It is one method, written out in full, with a worksheet and three emails. There is no theory section you have to get through first, and nothing here that only works if you have a budget.

Use it on something real. A pitch you have to win, a client you would love to work with, a business that has gone quiet. The method is boring when it has nothing to bite on.

---

## Part one — Why the obvious thing stops working

### The advice everyone gives you

Look at what is working. Do that.

It sounds unarguable. If something worked for someone else, why wouldn't it work for you?

So you do it. And it lands flat. Not badly — flat. It worked six months ago. It worked for them. Now it feels tired, or invisible, and you cannot say why.

### Why that happens

Two things are true about human attention at the same time, and they pull in opposite directions.

**We notice what is new.** Novelty might be a threat, so the brain flags it first. New things get looked at.

**We ignore what we recognise.** Once the brain has categorised something, it stops spending energy on it. Recognition is the beginning of being ignored.

Which means a trend works precisely because it is unfamiliar, and dies precisely when it becomes familiar. By the time you can see that something is working, you are already too late to be the one it works for.

That is not a failure of execution. It is biology.

### The trap this creates

You now have two bad options.

**Copy what works.** Safe, defensible, and invisible.

**Do something genuinely different.** Noticed, and risky. New things ask people to change their behaviour, and changing behaviour feels expensive. So the idea gets rejected — often by your own client, before it reaches a customer.

Advertising has always had a way out of this: money. If an idea is unfamiliar, you buy repetition until it stops being unfamiliar. Enough frequency turns strange into normal.

You probably do not have that. Freelancers, studios, founders and small teams cannot force behaviour change through spend. They have to earn it.

So the real question is this:

> **How do you make something new enough to get noticed, but familiar enough to get adopted?**

### The sweet spot

Think of a scale.

At one end, everything is familiar. You are wallpaper.

At the other, everything is new. You are a risk nobody wants to sign off.

The work that lands sits in the middle: **novelty anchored to something people already understand.**

Stranger Things is a new story told in a visual and emotional language its audience already knew by heart. The surprise is real. The comfort is real. Both at once.

The method in this document is one reliable way of reaching that middle without a Netflix budget.

## Part two — The idea in one line

> **Same audience. Different behaviour.**

Take the audience your client wants. Find another industry that audience already spends money with and already trusts. Borrow how that industry *behaves* and apply it to your client.

The novelty comes from the fact that nobody in your client's category behaves that way.

The familiarity comes from the fact that the audience has been fluent in that behaviour for years — just somewhere else.

**A run club inside a running brand is normal. A run club inside a drinks brand is interesting.** The behaviour has not changed. Only who is doing it.

### Three things this is not

**It is not copying your competitors.** That is the trap, not the escape.

**It is not a random mashup.** "A dentist behaving like a circus" is a joke, not a strategy, because there is no audience overlap holding it together. The overlap is the whole argument.

**It is not about aesthetics.** You are not borrowing a colour palette. You are borrowing behaviour: how they sell, what they sell, how they treat people, what rituals they build, how they make money, what they refuse to do.

### Why it de-risks a bold idea

This is the part that matters commercially, and it is why this works in a room rather than just on paper.

When you propose something genuinely new, the person opposite you is calculating risk. Nobody gets fired for the safe option.

But when you can say *"their customers already accept this from three brands they buy from every week"*, the idea stops being a gamble and becomes a transfer. You have given them a reason to say yes that survives being repeated to their boss.

## Part three — The method

Eleven steps. Work them in order the first time. After that you will skip around, and that is fine.

Each step has one question. The paragraph is context. **The question is the work.**

---

### 1. Who do they want to attract?

Not who they currently serve. Who they *wish* they served.

Choose an audience that is valuable to the business, credible for the brand to reach, and different enough from the current base that going after it counts as a decision rather than a description.

> **Which single audience would change this business if they won it?**

---

### 2. Where do those people already spend their money?

Do not guess, and do not describe them in demographics. Demographics tell you nothing about behaviour.

Follow the actual spending. Entertainment, fitness, food, travel, clothing, whatever it is. You are looking for categories that already have their attention and their card details.

> **List every category this audience already pays for without hesitating.**

---

### 3. Find the overlap

Narrow the list to four.

Three should be obvious enough that nobody in the room argues with them. One should be a wildcard that makes people sit up.

The wildcard is where the interesting work usually comes from. The obvious three are what make it defensible when someone asks why.

> **Three obvious industries, one wildcard. Write them down.**

---

### 4. Find the brands they already love

Audiences do not buy from industries. They buy from brands.

Name three to five specific ones in each category. Specific names, not sectors — "Rapha", not "cycling". Then ask what those brands have in common, and what could be borrowed from each.

> **Which named brands have already earned this audience's trust?**

---

### 5. Study how they behave

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that does the work.

Do not analyse everything. Collect only the behaviours that stand out, across four areas:

- **Behaviour** — how do they show up?
- **People** — who do they work with?
- **Experiences** — what do they create that people remember?
- **Ecosystem** — how do they make money, and how do they keep people coming back?

And use their *peak* behaviour, not their average. Every industry has a mediocre version. You want what it is known for at its best.

> **What does this brand do that your client's category would never think to do?**

---

### 6. Bring it back

Now ask the question the whole method has been building towards.

**Do not invent new ideas. Relocate existing ones.** The behaviour already works. It already has an audience. You are changing who performs it, not what it is.

> **What if our client behaved like this?**

---

### 7. Write the swap

Say it in one line: *a [X] behaving like a [Y]*.

If the line needs explaining, it is not the right line yet. The best ones are instantly picturable and slightly uncomfortable.

This line is what gets repeated when you are not in the room. Spend time on it.

> **Write the swap as a single sentence someone could repeat from memory.**

---

### 8. Stress test it

Three questions, honestly answered.

- **Is it valuable?** Would this audience actually matter to the client?
- **Is it credible?** Could this brand genuinely behave this way, or would it read as a costume?
- **Is it exciting?** Is there enough here to start a conversation?

> **Valuable, credible, exciting. Which one is weakest, and can you fix it?**

---

### 9. Build the world

Stop treating it as a campaign. Ask what would exist if this were simply how the business behaved.

Products. Experiences. Partnerships. Content. Community.

If the answers keep coming easily, you have found something worth building. **If you run dry after two, go back to step three and pick a different world.** That is not failure, it is the method working.

> **If this were the business rather than a campaign, what would naturally exist?**

---

### 10. Decide whether it is worth building

Before you spend real time bringing it to life: is it valuable, is it credible, is it deliverable, is it exciting?

If yes, go. If not, go back and pick a different world.

Abandoning a pairing at this stage costs you an afternoon. Abandoning it after the pitch costs you the client.

> **Would you bet your own money on this being commissioned?**

---

### 11. Make it feel real

You are not building the whole campaign. You are building belief.

Create just enough for someone to imagine the rest. A single image. A named product. One page.

Leave deliberate space for them to add to it. **If they start adding ideas of their own in the room, you have won** — because now it is partly theirs.

> **What is the smallest artefact that would make someone believe this could exist?**

## Part four — The worksheet

Once you have a pairing, fill this in. Eight blanks, one sitting. Print it, or copy it into a document.

> ### If a ________________ behaved like a ________________, they'd probably…

| | |
|---|---|
| **Sell** | |
| **Create experiences like** | |
| **Collaborate with** | |
| **Speak like** | |
| **Show up in** | |
| **Make money through** | |
| **Attract** | |
| **Stop doing** | |

That last one matters more than it looks. What a business refuses to do says as much as what it offers, and it is the line people repeat.

## Part five — Sending it

An idea is only worth having if it gets sent.

The tone that works is warm, short and unattached to the outcome. You are not pitching. You are sharing a thought you could not shake. Resist the urge to explain — the explanation is what makes it sound like a sales email.

### 1. The light touch

*Cold, or a long-dormant contact. Short enough to read on a phone.*

> **Subject: a thought about [company]**
>
> I had an idea about your business and it wouldn't leave me alone, so I'm sending it.
>
> What if [company] behaved like [Y]?
>
> [One sentence on what that would mean in practice.]
>
> Probably nothing, but I thought you'd find it interesting. Worth exploring?

### 2. The problem first

*When you know the friction their customers feel, and can name it.*

> **Subject: the bit everyone puts up with**
>
> Most people buying [category] quietly accept [the friction]. It's been that way long enough that nobody questions it any more.
>
> Meanwhile [Y industry] solved exactly this, years ago, with [the behaviour].
>
> So: [company] behaving like [Y]. [One sentence on what changes for the customer.]
>
> Shall we dig into it?

### 3. The reason to come back

*Keeping in touch. Send a new swap every few months to the same person.*

> **Subject: another one for you**
>
> Still thinking about [company]. This month's version:
>
> [X] behaving like [Y].
>
> [Two lines, no more.]
>
> No agenda. I just enjoy the puzzle, and you're one of the businesses I keep coming back to.

**That third one is the most valuable email in this document.** Most people have no legitimate reason to contact a prospect twice. This gives you a renewable one, forever, and it costs you an hour.
